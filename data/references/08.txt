A speech recognition system turns sound into text in several steps. First, the audio is split into short windows, about twenty-five milliseconds each, and every window is converted into a spectrogram, which shows how much energy the sound has at each frequency. The model reads these spectrograms and predicts a sequence of words, one piece at a time. To place each word on the timeline, a second step aligns the predicted text with the audio and estimates when every word starts and ends. These timestamps are useful, but they are not perfect. A small error of a fraction of a second can change how long a pause appears to be. That is why any measurement built on top of them, such as speaking speed or the number of pauses, should be reported with its limits. Good tools do not claim more accuracy than they really have. They show the number, explain how it was measured, and say where it might be wrong.
